# Literature review: does treatment delay raise cancer mortality?

_Papers reviewed: 3 | Model: muse-spark-1.3-contributor | Cited: 3/3_

This review is based only on the three provided abstracts [1][2][3].

Japanese mortality due to colorectal cancer surpassed 49,000 in 2015 [1]. The JSCCR Guidelines 2016 were prepared to show standard treatment strategies for colorectal cancer, to eliminate disparities among institutions in treatment, to eliminate unnecessary and insufficient treatment, and to deepen mutual understanding between health-care professionals and patients [1]. They were prepared by committee consensus based on literature review and in view of Japan's medical health insurance system and clinical practice settings, for use as a tool for treatment, informed consent, and choosing treatment method [1].

GLOBOCAN 2008 estimates reported about 12.7 million cancer cases and 7.6 million cancer deaths, with 56% of cases and 64% of deaths occurring in the economically developing world [2]. Breast cancer was the most frequently diagnosed cancer and the leading cause of cancer death among females, accounting for 23% of total cases and 14% of cancer deaths [2]. Lung cancer was the leading cancer site in males, comprising 17% of new cases and 23% of total cancer deaths [2].

GLOBOCAN estimates for 2012 reported about 14.1 million new cancer cases and 8.2 million deaths worldwide, with about 57% of cases and 65% of deaths in less developed countries [3]. Lung cancer was the leading cause of cancer death among males in both more and less developed countries and had surpassed breast cancer as the leading cause among females in more developed countries, while breast cancer remained the leading cause among females in less developed countries [3]. Other leading causes in more developed countries included colorectal cancer among males and females and prostate cancer among males [3].

On the research question of whether treatment delay raises cancer mortality, the provided papers do not support an answer [1][2][3]. None reports data on treatment delay, time to treatment, or the association of delay with mortality or survival [1][2][3].

Open questions:
1. Does time from diagnosis to treatment initiation affect cancer-specific mortality?
2. Does any effect of treatment delay differ by cancer site and stage?
3. What delay thresholds, if any, are associated with changes in mortality?
